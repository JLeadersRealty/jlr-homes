Acreages in 60 Seconds

An acreage isn't just a house with a bigger yard.

An acreage is a home on a few acres or more, usually outside town on a county road, with its own well or rural water, a private septic system, and room for outbuildings, animals or a big garden. In western Iowa, most acreages sit in the rolling farm and Loess Hills country of Pottawattamie, Harrison, Shelby, Mills and Cass counties, within 15 to 45 minutes of Council Bluffs and Omaha. Buying one means checking things a city home never asks about: water quality, the septic inspection Iowa requires at sale, road maintenance, fences, zoning and how to value land with few comparable sales.

Jamie's Insider Take
The #1 acreage surprise: Iowa's septic Time of Transfer inspection. Most Iowa acreages run on a private septic system, and it has to pass a certified inspection before the sale can close. If it fails, someone is paying for a repair or a new system, and that can run into the thousands. We get it scheduled early so nobody gets blindsided at the closing table.
Check the school district for the exact address. Out east of Council Bluffs, acreages can straddle the Underwood, Treynor and Lewis Central districts, sometimes on the same road.

Acreage FAQ

Questions about buying and selling acreages in Iowa

Does Iowa require a septic inspection when you sell an acreage?

Yes. Under Iowa's Time of Transfer law, a home served by a private septic system needs an inspection by a DNR-certified inspector before the sale closes. If the system is substandard, it has to be repaired or replaced, so it's smart to schedule the inspection early and agree up front on who pays for any fixes.

What is a groundwater hazard statement in Iowa?

It's a disclosure filed with the deed when a property is sold. The seller lists any known wells, private septic systems, underground storage tanks, hazardous waste, solid waste sites or private burial sites. If none exist, the deed includes a statement saying so instead.

How do you price an acreage when there aren't many comparable sales?

By hand. We look at land value, the home, outbuildings, water and septic, road access and the view, and we use acreage sales across a wider area, including ones that never got much online attention. Automated estimates usually miss on acreages because they can't see what makes one different.

Should I test the well before buying an acreage?

Yes. At minimum, test for bacteria and nitrates, and ask about the well's depth, age and flow rate. Your county's environmental health office can usually help with testing, and the results can affect both your financing and your offer.

Can I use a USDA loan to buy an acreage?

Often, yes. Many rural addresses in western Iowa qualify for USDA Rural Development loans, which can mean little or no down payment. Eligibility depends on the address and your income, and some lenders have limits on acreage size or outbuildings, so talk to a lender early.

Who pays for a boundary fence on an Iowa acreage?

Under Iowa's partition fence law, neighboring landowners can be required to share the cost of building and maintaining a boundary fence. Ask the seller about fence condition and any existing agreements with neighbors before you buy.
