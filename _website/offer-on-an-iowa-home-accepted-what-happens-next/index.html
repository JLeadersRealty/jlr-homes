Offer on an Iowa Home Accepted? What Happens Next

By Jamie Leaders, Broker/OwnerNovember 14, 20269 min read

What happens after you accept an offer on an Iowa home?

After you accept an offer on an Iowa home, you move into a contract-driven window: the buyer's inspection, the lender's appraisal and underwriting, the title review, and closing all run on the dates in your signed purchase agreement. In Iowa, the title step centers on the abstract of title, which has to be updated and extended, and that can take several weeks. Start it right away so it doesn't hold up everything else.

Key Takeaways

  • The abstract of title extension can take several weeks, so it should start the day after you accept the offer, not after the inspection wraps up.
  • Inspection, appraisal, financing-commitment, title-objection, possession, and closing dates all come from your signed purchase agreement, not from a standard Iowa calendar.
  • The buyer's attorney, or an attorney engaged by the buyer's lender, reviews the abstract and issues a title opinion on whether you have marketable title, and that opinion is ordinarily the buyer's expense.
  • An unreleased mortgage, a judgment, an estate issue, or a boundary matter can trigger cure work before closing, so you want to find it early.
  • Council Bluffs had a median sale price of $232,500 over the trailing 90 days, as of November 2026, according to aggregated public listing data, but that number says nothing about how long your own closing will take.

A signed contract feels like the finish line. I tell my clients it's really the point where the real work starts. Once you've accepted an offer on an Iowa home, a handful of milestones start running at the same time, and the sellers who stay calm are the ones who know the order of operations before it begins.

What should you do first after accepting an offer on an Iowa home?

Find your abstract of title and arrange for an abstract company to update and extend it. According to the Iowa State Bar Association, the extension can take several weeks, which is why it can't wait until the other steps are done.

What is the abstract, and why does Iowa still use it?

The abstract is a compiled history of the public-record documents that affect your property's title: deeds, mortgages, court records, and other governmental filings. Iowa commonly relies on an abstract-and-attorney title-opinion process rather than leaning only on a title-insurance search.

Once the abstract is extended, it goes to the buyer's attorney, or in many financed purchases to an attorney engaged by the buyer's lender. That attorney issues a title opinion on whether you can deliver marketable title. The Iowa State Bar Association identifies that title opinion as ordinarily being the buyer's expense, but responsibilities can shift depending on the purchase agreement and the financing, so confirm yours in your contract.

What if you can't find your abstract?

This is the first snag I see. Maybe it's in a file cabinet, a safe deposit box, or with a prior lender or the person you bought from. Track it down in the first couple of days. If the previous holder has it, you need to know that now, not in week three.

Who pays for the abstract extension?

The seller normally arranges the update and extension, but who ultimately bears the cost is something your purchase agreement and closing arrangements spell out. I'd rather you read that clause on day one than discover it at closing. If you want to talk through your specific contract language, that's exactly what a consultation is for.

How do inspection, appraisal, and loan approval work after acceptance?

They're three separate checkpoints, each with its own purpose and its own deadline in your contract. The abstract extension usually runs at the same time as all of them, which is why starting it early matters.

The inspection looks at condition

An inspection evaluates the property's physical condition for the buyer. If it turns up a problem, the buyer may ask for repairs, a credit, or a price change, and you decide how to respond. Whether the buyer can walk away, and by when, depends on the inspection contingency in your signed agreement. Once that deadline passes, the buyer's ability to cancel over condition issues generally narrows, but other contingencies, like financing or title, may still be open. Read your contract rather than assuming.

If you're weighing whether to fix things or let the buyer handle them, I broke that decision down in Repair Before You Sell, or List As-Is in Council Bluffs?

If the property has a well or septic system, those inspections can add their own findings. Rural sellers should know what a buyer is likely to check, and I covered that in Well & Septic Inspections for Iowa & Nebraska Acreage Buyers.

The appraisal looks at value

The appraisal is for the buyer's lender, and it asks one question: does the property support the loan at the contract price? A clean inspection doesn't guarantee an appraisal at or above your price, and an appraisal that matches your price doesn't mean the home is free of defects. They answer different questions.

If an appraisal comes in below the agreed price, the deal isn't automatically dead. The usual paths are renegotiating the price, the buyer covering the gap, or one side ending the contract if the agreement allows it. Which of those is available to you depends on the appraisal and financing language you both signed.

Final loan approval follows the paperwork

The buyer's lender is working through underwriting while the inspection and title review happen. Final approval typically comes after the lender has the appraisal and the title opinion in hand, and your contract sets the financing-commitment date. If that date gets close and you haven't heard anything, ask your agent to check in with the buyer's side. Don't wait for silence to turn into a surprise.

What happens if the title work finds a problem, and what do you sign at closing?

If the title opinion flags a defect, you'll typically have a window to cure it, and the exact deadline and the buyer's right to terminate depend on your signed contract. If it's clean, you move on to closing paperwork.

What kinds of title problems show up?

  • An unreleased mortgage that was paid off years ago but never formally released.
  • A judgment or lien recorded against you or a prior owner.
  • An estate issue, such as a deed from someone who passed without probate being completed.
  • A boundary matter or another recorded instrument that clouds ownership.

Curing these can mean obtaining releases, corrective instruments, or probate documents. That's slow work, which is another reason the abstract goes first. If the home came to you through an estate, read Selling an Inherited House in Iowa or Nebraska before you list, because those issues are best caught before a buyer is in the picture.

What does an Iowa seller sign at closing?

The seller is normally responsible for preparing or supplying the transfer documents. Per the Iowa State Bar Association, the usual package includes:

  • The deed
  • The Ground Water Hazard Statement
  • The Declaration of Value
  • The closing statement

The exact documents and signatures depend on the property, how it's owned, the buyer's lender, and your contract. Your closing agent will tell you what applies to your file.

Iowa also has a real estate transfer tax under Iowa Code chapter 428A, set at $0.80 for each $500, or fractional part of $500, of value above $500, with statutory exemptions. It's collected at recording, and county recorders follow Department of Revenue procedures for declarations of value under Iowa Administrative Code chapter 701-109. How that charge is allocated between buyer and seller is a matter for your purchase agreement and closing arrangements, so it isn't automatically yours. Confirm it in your contract.

What happens after closing?

The buyer or the buyer's lender generally records the deed, Declaration of Value, and Ground Water Hazard Statement after closing, and the buyer or lender keeps the abstract. Your part is mostly done once you've signed and handed over possession as your contract describes.

What does this look like in Council Bluffs and the Omaha metro?

Council Bluffs sits in Pottawattamie County, so the Iowa side of the metro follows Iowa law and Iowa recording procedures, even though the buyer pool and the lenders often cross the river. Keep the market numbers in perspective: the metro is bigger than any one city, and so are its averages.

For context, the Omaha and Council Bluffs metro (both the Nebraska and Iowa portions) had an average listing price of $467,007 in September 2026, according to the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis. FAQ

What should I do immediately after accepting an offer on my Iowa house?

Locate your abstract of title and arrange for an abstract company to update and extend it. The extension can take several weeks, so starting right away keeps it from delaying closing. Then put every deadline from your signed contract on a calendar.

How long does an Iowa abstract update usually take?

It can take several weeks, according to the Iowa State Bar Association. The exact time depends on the abstract company's workload and whether your abstract is easy to find and in good order. It typically runs at the same time as the inspection, appraisal, and underwriting.

What happens if the buyer's inspection finds a problem after acceptance?

The buyer can usually ask for repairs, a credit, or a price change, and you decide whether to agree. Whether the buyer can cancel, and by when, depends on the inspection contingency in your purchase agreement. Respond within the contract's timeline, not after it.

What happens if the appraisal comes in below the agreed price?

The deal isn't automatically over, but you'll need to decide how to proceed. Common paths include renegotiating the price, the buyer making up the difference, or ending the contract if your agreement allows. The available options depend on your contract's appraisal and financing terms.

What happens if the abstract shows a lien or another title problem?

You'll typically need to cure it before closing, such as by obtaining a mortgage release, a corrective instrument, or probate documents. The deadline and the buyer's right to terminate depend on your signed contract. Finding it early gives you the most time to fix it.
