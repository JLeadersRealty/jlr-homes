1031 Exchange Basics for Iowa and Nebraska Investors

By Jamie Leaders, Broker/OwnerOctober 2, 20265 min read

What Is a 1031 Exchange and How Does It Work?

A 1031 exchange lets you sell an investment property and roll the proceeds into another "like-kind" investment property without paying capital gains tax on the sale right now — you defer it instead. You have 45 days from closing on the sale to identify replacement property and 180 days total to close on it, and the money has to pass through a qualified intermediary, never through your own hands. Miss either deadline and the exchange falls apart, and the tax bill comes due.

If you own a rental in Council Bluffs, a duplex in Omaha, or acreage you've been leasing out, and you're thinking about selling and moving that equity into something bigger or better positioned, a 1031 exchange is probably a term you've already heard. Here's what it actually requires, because the deadlines are unforgiving and the paperwork trips up more investors than the concept itself does.

The Two Deadlines That Make or Break the Exchange

These are the two numbers to have memorized before you sell anything:

- 45 days from the closing date of your relinquished property to formally identify your replacement property (or properties) in writing to your qualified intermediary.

- 180 days from that same closing date to actually close on the replacement property.

Both clocks start the moment your sale closes — not when you start looking for the next property. There's no extension for "still under contract" or "the seller pushed the closing date." If you're planning a 1031 exchange, the identification and replacement search needs to start before you list the property you're selling, not after.

What Counts as "Like-Kind" Real Estate

For real estate, "like-kind" is broader than most investors expect. It doesn't mean you have to trade a single-family rental for another single-family rental. You can exchange:

- A single-family rental in Council Bluffs for a small multi-family property in Omaha

- Leased acreage for a commercial building

- One investment property for several smaller ones (or several for one), as long as the total value and equity requirements are met

The property just has to be held for investment or business use on both ends — a personal residence, a second home you use yourself, or property held primarily for resale (like a flip) doesn't qualify.

One thing that hasn't changed: the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA), signed in 2025, did not touch 1031 exchange rules for real property. The framework here is the same one that's been in place since the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act narrowed 1031 exchanges to real estate only back in 2018 — personal property like equipment and vehicles lost eligibility then, and that hasn't changed since.

Why Investors' Exchanges Actually Fail

It's rarely the concept that trips people up — it's the execution. The most common failure points:

1. Touching the money. If sale proceeds land in your account, even briefly, the exchange is disqualified. Everything has to route through a qualified intermediary (QI) who holds the funds and handles the paperwork between closings.

2. Missing the 45-day identification window. This one sneaks up fast, especially in a market with limited inventory. Line up your replacement property options before you close on the sale, not after.

3. Paperwork and identification errors. The IRS has specific rules about how many properties you can identify and how they need to be described in writing. A QI experienced with 1031s handles this, but it's worth understanding before you're mid-transaction.

4. Related-party transactions. Exchanging with a family member or a business partner is possible but comes with extra scrutiny and a two-year holding requirement — loop in your tax advisor early if this applies to you.

Why This Matters for Investors in This Market

I talk to investors regularly who are sitting on a property that's appreciated well and want to move that equity into something with better cash flow, less maintenance, or more room to grow — trading a single rental for a small multi-unit, or repositioning out of a property that's become more work than it's worth. A 1031 exchange is often how that move pencils out, because paying capital gains tax on the sale first can eat into the down payment you'd need for the next purchase.

That said, this is general education, not tax advice — your specific numbers depend on your basis, depreciation recapture, and overall tax situation, and a 1031 exchange only makes sense as part of a plan you've run past your CPA and a qualified intermediary before you list anything. Where I come in is helping you find the right replacement property in the right window and structuring the sale side so the timeline actually works — that's exactly the kind of thing I walk investors through before we even talk about listing.

Frequently Asked Questions

What qualifies as "like-kind" property in a 1031 exchange?

Almost any real estate held for investment or business use qualifies as like-kind to any other — a rental house can exchange for a commercial building, acreage, or a multi-family property. It doesn't have to be the same property type, but it can't be a personal residence or property held primarily for resale.

What happens if I miss the 45-day identification deadline?

The exchange fails, and the sale is treated as a normal taxable transaction — capital gains tax applies as if no exchange happened. There's no extension available, which is why lining up replacement options before you close on the sale matters so much.

Do I need a qualified intermediary?

Yes. Sale proceeds can never pass through your hands — they have to be held by a qualified intermediary between the sale and the purchase. Skipping this step disqualifies the exchange entirely.

Can I 1031 exchange a Council Bluffs property into a Nebraska property, or vice versa?

Yes. A 1031 exchange isn't limited by state lines — you can exchange investment property in Iowa for investment property in Nebraska or anywhere else in the country, as long as both properties qualify as like-kind investment real estate.

Did anything change for 1031 exchanges in 2026?

No. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act, passed in 2025, left the rules for real property exchanges untouched. The 45-day and 180-day deadlines and the like-kind requirements remain what they've been since 2018.
